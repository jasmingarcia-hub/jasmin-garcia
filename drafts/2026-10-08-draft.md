# Global Dental Workforce and Basic Oral-Health Service Availability

## A problem and why it matters

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that approximately 3.5 billion people were affected by oral diseases, with three out of four affected people living in low- and middle-income countries. The burden falls disproportionately on disadvantaged populations (WHO, 2022c). In 2022, the World Health Assembly adopted a global strategy envisioning universal health coverage for oral health by 2030 (WHO, 2022a, 2022c).

Dental care depends on trained labor. This paper asks how dentist density, measured as dentists per 10,000 people, is associated with basic oral-health service availability across countries at different income levels. Workforce supply, training barriers, financing, and incentives to serve underserved populations are the economic questions behind this comparison.

## My interpretation of the evidence

The analysis matches 171 countries using WHO dentist density, World Bank income groups, and three WHO 2021 service indicators: screening, urgent oral care, and basic restorative care (WHO, n.d.-b; World Bank, n.d.). The outcome measures general availability in public primary-care facilities, defined as reaching at least half of patients in need. Falling below that threshold does not mean no care exists (WHO, 2022b, p. 2).

“Not achieved” means none of the three services meets that public-primary-care threshold. This category includes 7 of 55 high-income countries (12.7%), compared with 3 of 53 upper-middle-income countries (5.7%). It does not mean there is no dental care, and these data cannot show whether private delivery explains a country's result. The dentist-density correlation therefore describes reported public-primary-care availability, rather than overall access across public and private providers.

Dentist supply and service availability had a modest relationship (Spearman rank correlation 0.306). Median density, or the middle value, was 2.97 where all three services were generally available, 0.76 where none met that criterion, and 0.72 where one or two did. It did not rise steadily across categories (Figure 1; Appendix Table A1).

Income was closely related to dentist supply. Median density rose from 0.06 in low-income countries to 0.31 in lower-middle-income, 2.33 in upper-middle-income, and 7.39 in high-income countries (Figure 2; Appendix Table A2). Using workforce observations from 2021 or earlier, within-group correlations were 0.374 in low-income, 0.098 in lower-middle-income, −0.170 in upper-middle-income, and 0.179 in high-income countries. The upper-middle-income relationship was weak and negative, rather than absent; the positive relationship was largest in the low-income group. The varying patterns do not isolate income's influence or establish a causal workforce effect.

I repeated the analysis using each country's latest dentist observation from 2021 or earlier. The correlation barely changed, from 0.306 to 0.305, retaining all 171 countries. Restricting workforce observations to 2018–2021 retained 157 countries and gave 0.325. On those same 157 countries, the original observations through 2023 gave 0.340. The modest positive relationship therefore persisted in both timing checks; the recent-window comparison also changes which countries are included.

Some dentist observations remain older than 2021. Reporting differences, today's income groups, and the gap between availability and actual access remain concerns. Sources may count working or registered dentists and vary in completeness (WHO, n.d.-a). Country comparisons cannot prove cause and effect: income, health-system design, and spending could influence both supply and availability. National averages can hide local shortages. The analysis cannot show whether financing, provider location, or sharing tasks with other trained workers explains the differences (Appendix Table A3).

I expected dentist scarcity to be the main explanation. The findings challenged that expectation without establishing a better explanation.

Of the 171 countries in the main sample, 99 reported whether essential dental treatment was included in their largest public scheme: 78 answered Yes and 21 answered No. The other 72 had no usable response to this question; they were not counted as No. All three basic services were generally available in 49 of the 78 Yes countries and 9 of the 21 No countries (Table 1).

Table 1. Essential-curative benefit inclusion and basic service availability (2021)

| Benefit included in largest public scheme | Countries | All three services generally available | 95% Wilson interval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yes | 78 | 49 (62.8%) | 51.7–72.7% |

The two-sided Fisher exact test gives p = 0.135, providing no strong statistical evidence of a difference. Patterns vary by income group, some subgroups are small, and median dentist density using observations through 2021 is higher where the benefit is included (2.23 versus 0.72). The test and intervals cannot separate financing from income or supply, or account for which countries reported data.

## Policy options and my provisional judgment

I am considering three options:

1. Expand dentist training and retention. This could increase capacity, but requires money and time; graduates may still choose affluent or urban areas to work in.

2. Improve provider location and links with primary care. This could help existing providers reach underserved populations, but requires ongoing recruitment, retention, referrals, and public-sector support.

3. Expand public financing for essential dental care. This could make care more affordable, but additional demand may exceed provider capacity. The financing comparison highlights affordability and capacity together, without proving a provider shortage.

Recommendation. For national ministries of health, working with public health-care payers and workforce planners, I recommend matching policy to workforce capacity and patients' barriers. The income differences, modest workforce relationship, and mixed financing results inform this tentative judgment; they do not show how many dentists are enough or which policy works best. Where local evidence confirms severe shortages, I would prioritize training, retention, and helping providers reach underserved areas. Where providers can deliver care but cost is a major barrier, I would prioritize financing that reduces patients' costs, pays providers enough to participate, and encourages care in underserved areas.

The strongest objection. My country comparisons cannot prove that financing improves access. Income or workforce capacity could explain both coverage and availability; the data cannot show which came first. Romania covers essential dental treatment in its largest public scheme, yet none of the three basic services is reported as generally available in public primary-care facilities (WHO, 2022b). Only 5% of dental spending was publicly funded in 2021 (OECD/European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies, 2023, p. 15). Listing a benefit therefore differs from reducing patients' costs. This share does not tell us who receives care or measure a reform's effect, and the availability measure may miss private treatment. Subsidies could raise prices or waiting times if providers cannot deliver more care; this is a theoretical risk, not my finding.

My response. My findings show a relationship, but do not prove cause and effect. My recommendation depends on local conditions. Public payments and patients' share of costs should make care affordable and encourage provider participation. Low payments can discourage participation, but my evidence does not establish this as Romania's cause. Incentives to serve underserved areas may also be needed. I would test reforms on a small scale, comparing costs with care received, affordability, unmet need, and oral health. I would reconsider if credible evaluations found no improvement after benefits expanded, or showed that workforce expansion or better provider distribution worked better at similar supply levels.
