### The problem and why it matters

WHO's 2022 report estimated that approximately 3.5 billion people—almost half the world's population—were affected by oral diseases, with three out of four affected people living in low- and middle-income countries. These figures highlight substantial unmet needs for prevention and treatment (WHO, 2022b).

Dental care depends on trained labor, making dentist supply a reasonable starting point for investigating constraints on service availability. This paper asks how dentist workforce density, measured as dentists per 10,000 people, is associated with oral-health service availability across countries at different income levels. It also examines countries that depart from the overall pattern.

WHO reported that global cases of oral diseases had increased by one billion over the preceding 30 years. The burden was disproportionately high among people with low incomes, people with disabilities, older people living alone or in care homes, rural and remote communities, and minority groups (WHO, 2022b).

The 2022 Global Oral Health Status Report provided a comprehensive assessment of oral disease burden, accompanied by profiles for 194 countries. That year, the World Health Assembly adopted a global strategy envisioning universal health coverage for oral health by 2030 (WHO, 2022a, 2022b). Pursuing that goal raises economic questions about workforce supply, training barriers, financing, and incentives for providers to serve underserved populations.

### My interpretation of the evidence

The analysis of 171 countries showed a modest positive association between dentist density and reported availability of basic oral-health services. Income was also closely associated with dentist supply.

| Reported basic service availability | Median dentists per 10,000 people |
|---|---:|
| None of the three services available | 0.76 |
| One or two services available | 0.72 |
| All three services available | 2.97 |

The three services are oral-health screening, urgent oral care, and basic restorative care. The categories describe reported availability in public primary-care facilities; they do not indicate whether these services exist elsewhere in a country.

Countries reporting all three services had a higher median dentist density. However, countries reporting no services and partial availability had nearly identical medians. The results therefore did not show a steady increase in dentist density across the three availability categories.

| World Bank income group | Median dentists per 10,000 people |
|---|---:|
| Low income | 0.06 |
| Lower middle income | 0.31 |
| Upper middle income | 2.33 |
| High income | 7.39 |

Source for both tables: calculations from the matched WHO–World Bank dataset, documented in `data/research_analysis_summary.md`.

Within the two middle-income groups, dentist density had almost no association with service availability. This is consistent with income accounting for part of the overall pattern, although these comparisons do not isolate the effect of income or eliminate other explanations.

Restricting workforce observations to 2018–2023 produced a similar overall association. However, this checks workforce-data recency rather than alignment with the service-availability data, which are from 2021.

I interpret these findings as evidence that dentist supply and service availability are related, but national dentist numbers alone do not explain the differences between countries. I expected dentist scarcity to be the dominant explanation. The findings made me question that expectation without establishing a better explanation.

| What we cannot conclude | Why |
|---|---|
| More dentists cause better service availability | The analysis shows associations, not cause and effect. |
| Dentist supply does not matter | A weak national relationship can conceal local shortages or differences in how dentists work. |
| Financing, deployment, or task sharing explains the differences | These possible explanations have not yet been tested. |
| Patients actually receive affordable, accessible care | The outcome measures reported service availability in public primary care, not utilization, cost, travel distance, or quality. |
| A particular policy would improve access | The analysis has not evaluated the effects, costs, or tradeoffs of an intervention. |

Differences in reporting, observation years, and income-classification timing also limit confidence in the interpretation.

### Policy options and my provisional judgment

I am considering three policy options:

1. **Expand dentist training and retention.** This could increase workforce capacity, but it requires money and time. Additional graduates may still choose affluent or urban areas rather than underserved communities.

2. **Improve deployment and primary-care integration.** This could help existing providers reach underserved populations. Implementation would require attention to practice location, public-sector participation, referral arrangements, and retention. Recruitment or integration efforts may require ongoing resources and may not produce lasting changes in where providers work.

3. **Expand public financing for essential dental care.** This could reduce patients' financial barriers. However, increased demand may exceed available provider capacity.

My provisional preference is to expand public financing for essential dental care where affordability is a major barrier and providers have capacity to deliver treatment. This preference draws on WHO's discussion of out-of-pocket costs, rather than evidence that my country-level analysis has established a financing effect. WHO identifies financial barriers, limited primary-care integration, and information gaps as obstacles to oral-health care (WHO, 2022b).

Evidence that patients cannot afford otherwise available care would strengthen this preference. Two findings could shift my priority:

- If funded services cannot recruit providers because there are too few providers overall, I would place greater emphasis on training and retention.
- If the national workforce is sufficient but underserved areas cannot attract or retain providers, I would place greater emphasis on deployment incentives.

Policy evaluations showing improvements in treatment received, affordability, or oral-health outcomes relative to their costs would help me compare these alternatives. Such evidence would be more informative for choosing a policy than country-level correlations alone.

## Working Title

Global Dental Workforce Scarcity and Access to Oral Health Care

## Research Question

How is dentist workforce density associated with access to oral health care across countries with different income levels?

## Working Hypothesis

Countries with lower income levels will generally have fewer dentists per 10,000 population and may also have weaker access to oral health services. However, dentist density alone is unlikely to fully explain access. Financing systems, geographic distribution, training capacity, and workforce policy may help explain why some countries perform better or worse than expected.

## Economic Framework

This project will use several economic concepts to explain the global dental workforce challenge:

- **Scarcity:** Dentists are a limited health-care resource, and scarcity is greater where the number of providers is low relative to population.
- **Labor supply:** The available number of trained dentists reflects education capacity, migration, compensation, working conditions, and professional incentives.
- **Human capital:** Training dentists requires substantial time and investment, which limits how quickly supply can expand.
- **Barriers to entry:** Educational requirements, licensing, infrastructure, and the cost of training can restrict entry into the profession.
- **Geographic maldistribution:** A country may have an adequate national dentist-to-population ratio while still having severe shortages in rural or underserved areas.
- **Incentives:** Compensation, loan support, training opportunities, public-sector employment, and other incentives may affect where dentists choose to practice.
- **Income and ability to finance care:** National income and health-financing systems may affect both the supply of dentists and patients' ability to obtain services.

## Planned Data

The analysis will combine three country-level measures:

1. **Dentist workforce density** — dentists per 10,000 population, from WHO.
2. **Country income** — GDP per capita and/or World Bank income classification.
3. **Oral-health access** — WHO's 2023 `ORALHEALTH_SERVICESPHCFACILITIES` classification of oral-health services generally available in primary health care facilities: not achieved, partially achieved, or fully achieved. The three underlying 2021 screening, urgent-care, and restorative-care indicators will be retained as supporting variables.

## Planned Analysis

The first analysis will compare dentist density with country income level. The second will examine whether higher dentist density is associated with better oral-health access. Countries that do not follow the overall pattern will be examined as possible counterexamples.

The primary figure will compare dentist density across WHO-style oral-health service-availability categories:

- **Not achieved:** none of the three services generally available
- **Partially achieved:** one or two services generally available
- **Fully achieved:** all three services generally available

A grouped dot/strip plot or box plot is more appropriate than a conventional scatterplot because the access measure is ordinal rather than continuous. Country income level will be incorporated by category, symbol, facet, or a supporting figure.

The paper will avoid treating correlation as proof of causation. Instead, the results will be used to evaluate whether workforce scarcity appears to be an important part of the access problem and which additional mechanisms may matter.

## Policy Question

If countries with limited dentist supply also show weaker access, what workforce policies are most defensible for improving access?

Possible policy areas to evaluate after the data are reviewed include:

- expanding dental education and training capacity
- incentives for service in underserved areas
- public-sector workforce investment
- retention strategies and migration-related incentives
- task sharing or broader use of allied oral-health personnel
- financing reforms that increase effective demand for preventive and routine care

The final recommendation will depend on the observed evidence rather than being selected in advance.

## Current Limitations

WHO workforce observations are not necessarily from the same year across countries, and coverage is incomplete. Cross-country comparisons will therefore need to distinguish association from causation and acknowledge differences in data timing, health systems, geography, and reporting quality.

## Access Indicator Decision — October 2, 2026

The primary access measure will use the WHO NCD Country Capacity Survey indicators for availability of oral-health services in primary care rather than relying on the percentage of the population covered by the largest government health financing scheme.

The three component services are:

1. oral-health screening for early detection of oral diseases
2. urgent treatment for emergency oral care and pain relief
3. basic restorative dental procedures to treat existing dental decay

Each country will receive a transparent 0–3 score based on the number of these services reported as generally available. This matches the structure of WHO Global Oral Health Action Plan Core Indicator 4.1, which classifies countries as not achieved, partially achieved, or fully achieved based on the same three services.

This measure was selected because it is more directly related to actual service availability than financing-scheme enrollment. The financing data will remain useful as a secondary measure, but scheme coverage alone can overstate oral-health access when oral-health interventions are excluded from the benefit package.

The main limitation is that this is a country-reported availability measure, not a direct measure of patient utilization, affordability, quality, or geographic access within countries.


## Data Design Update — October 3, 2026

WHO now reports the Global Oral Health Action Plan Core Indicator 4.1 directly at the country level through GHO indicator `ORALHEALTH_SERVICESPHCFACILITIES`. This is a cleaner primary outcome than constructing a new 0–3 score because WHO has already applied the official classification rules.

For the matched dataset, the analysis will therefore use:
- WHO 2023 oral-health service-availability category as the primary outcome;
- the most recent WHO dentist-density observation available on or before 2023, with its observation year retained;
- World Bank income group and 2023 GDP per capita;
- the three 2021 WHO oral-health service components as supporting variables where available.

This change improves reproducibility and avoids creating an unnecessary researcher-defined index. The next audit step is to run the data extraction and report how many countries have overlapping access, dentist-density, and World Bank income data.


## Data Assembly Results — October 3, 2026

The reproducible WHO–World Bank merge produced **171 countries with complete primary analysis fields**: an oral-health service-availability category, dentist workforce density, and World Bank income group.

Matched sample by oral-health access category:
- Not achieved: 23 countries
- Partially achieved: 37 countries
- Fully achieved: 111 countries

Matched sample by World Bank income group:
- Low income: 21 countries
- Lower middle income: 42 countries
- Upper middle income: 53 countries
- High income: 55 countries

The direct 2023 WHO aggregate access series is documented in the Global Oral Health Action Plan monitoring framework but did not expose usable country rows through the GHO API during extraction. Therefore, where the direct category was unavailable, the dataset applies WHO's official Core Indicator 4.1 classification rules to the three underlying WHO service-availability components. A category is derived only when all three component responses are reported; missing responses are not coded as unavailable.

Dentist-density observations in the matched sample range from 2004 to 2023. However, 159 of the 171 matched countries have dentist-density observations from 2018–2023, 169 have observations from 2013–2023, and only 2 have observations older than 2013. The main analysis can therefore use the full matched sample, with a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 159 countries whose workforce observations are within five years of the 2023 target year.


## Initial Findings — October 3, 2026

The first cross-country analysis shows a positive but modest association between dentist workforce density and WHO primary-care oral-health service availability.

Across the 171-country matched sample:
- Not achieved countries have a median of **0.76 dentists per 10,000 population**.
- Partially achieved countries have a median of **0.72 dentists per 10,000 population**.
- Fully achieved countries have a median of **2.97 dentists per 10,000 population**.
- The Spearman rank correlation between dentist density and access is approximately **0.31**.

The 2018–2023 workforce-data sensitivity sample produces a nearly identical result (Spearman ρ ≈ **0.33**), so the broad finding is not being driven by the small number of older dentist observations.

Country income shows a much stronger relationship with dentist supply. Median dentist density rises from **0.06** in low-income countries to **7.39** in high-income countries, and the Spearman correlation between income group and dentist density is approximately **0.74**.

Full achievement of the WHO access measure also rises sharply with income:
- Low income: **38.1%**
- Lower middle income: **42.9%**
- Upper middle income: **77.4%**
- High income: **80.0%**

Within the lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income groups, the relationship between dentist density and access is essentially zero. This suggests that the overall cross-country association is partly confounded by income and that dentist supply alone does not determine whether basic oral-health services are available through primary care.

Two useful counterexamples reinforce that point. The Central African Republic reports extremely low dentist density but all three WHO primary-care service components as available. Romania has a relatively high dentist density but reports all three components as unavailable in public-sector primary care. These examples are consistent with the idea that workforce size and service-system organization are related but distinct.

The emerging policy implication is therefore not simply "train more dentists." Countries with severe workforce scarcity may need more training and retention capacity, but improvements in access may also depend on how oral-health services are financed, integrated into primary care, geographically deployed, and supported by allied personnel.
