# Research Brief — Global Dental Workforce and Access to Oral Health Care

## The problem

A global economic concern to explore is how an unequal distribution of dentists across countries can affect access to oral health care. Dentist scarcity can be measured as the number of dentists per 10,000 population. Lower dentist density may be associated with reduced access. The research will test this relationship rather than assume that lower dentist density automatically causes reduced access.

## Research Question and Economic Concepts

**How is dentist workforce density associated with access to oral health care across countries with different income levels?**

The project will examine concepts such as scarcity, labor supply, incentives, human capital, barriers to entry/training, geographic maldistribution, and country income levels. I will evaluate how dentist workforce density varies across countries at different income levels. I will also examine whether lower dentist density is associated with reduced access to oral health services. Scarcity may also affect labor-market incentives, including dentist compensation and the ability of underserved areas to attract and retain providers.

## Planned Analysis

The analysis will compare three variables:

1. **Dentist workforce density** — number of dentists per 10,000 population
2. **Country income level** — for example, GDP per capita or World Bank income category
3. **Oral-health access** — using an internationally comparable measure of oral-health service access that I will identify and verify during the research stage

The analysis should help determine which workforce policies or strategies could improve access in countries where dental workforce supply is limited. I will use dentists relative to population rather than raw dentist counts. Based on the relationship between workforce supply and access, I will identify which policies appear most appropriate for countries with limited dentist availability.

## Hypothesis and Policy Question

I expect lower-income countries to generally have fewer dentists relative to their populations and potentially weaker access to oral health services, but the research will also examine whether there are countries that do not follow this pattern. I will evaluate what workforce policies could improve access, which approaches appear most appropriate where dentist supply is limited, and what might explain why countries perform better or worse than expected.


## What I am assuming

I am assuming that national dentist density, measured as dentists per 10,000 people, is a meaningful proxy for the supply of dental care, even though a national average does not show where dentists practice, how many hours they work, or what they charge. I am assuming that WHO service-availability reporting accurately reflects whether oral health services are actually available in each country, and that availability is a reasonable stand-in for access, even though access also depends on affordability, distance, and demand. I am assuming that countries define and report both dentists and service availability consistently enough for cross-country comparison, and that differences in definitions, reporting years, and data quality are small enough not to drive the results. I am assuming that national income is an important confounder because it may influence both dentist supply and service availability. Comparing countries within similar income groups may help account for this, although it does not eliminate other explanations. Finally, I am assuming that governments can influence dental workforce supply and deployment, while recognizing that financing, prices, and other barriers may also affect access.

Which assumption needs the most testing

The assumption that needs the most testing, is the comparability of the reporting and observation years because we want to know whether the patterns reflect real differences between countries or differences in how and when the data were collected. 

My conclusion could change if: 
- I compared countries using more consistent definitions and data from the same period.
- Income gradient is weaker than expected and the lower-income countries do not clearly have lower density, or the gap disappears once I account for the reporting-year mismatch.
- Outliers drive the result and the association vanishes when we drop a handful of countries or a rank-based measure (Speearman) teslls a different story that Pearson.
- Income classification dates matter (for example, countries that changed income group between the classification used and 2021 account for much of the pattern).

## How I would know I was wrong

I would be wrong if the positive association between dentist density and service availability weakens substantially or disappears when I restrict dentist observations to those on or before 2021, and again when I restrict them to 2018-2021, since that would suggest the original pattern came from mismatched years rather than real differences between countries. I would also be wrong if the countries that look most surprising, such as Romania and the Central African Republic, turn out to reflect differences in definitions or reporting practices rather than real differences in supply, because that would mean the data cannot support the country-level comparisons I intend to draw. Finally, I would be wrong if the association depends on current income classifications and changes when I use classifications appropriate to 2021 or 2021 GDP per capita. This assumption was identified after I had seen preliminary findings, in which dentist observations from 2022-2023 were paired with a 2021 service-availability measure; I therefore treat the year-alignment checks as exploratory sensitivity tests rather than as tests specified before the analysis began
